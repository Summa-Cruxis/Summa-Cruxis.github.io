How it compares

Built to remember people, not to collect them.

Most tools that touch business cards are built around a platform, a team or a sales pipeline. CardKeep is built for one person who meets people and wants to remember them, privately.

Real-card reading test

On October 6, 2026 we scanned 11 real business cards once each in CardKeep (beta build 279, with AI card reading allowed) and in a competing card-scanner app (free tier). We saved each first result without corrections and checked every printed detail against the card itself.

The cards came from Japan, the UK, Italy, Costa Rica, the Turks and Caicos Islands and the US. They included all-caps print, vertical layouts, logo badges, extensions, toll-free numbers, two hotels on one card and a QR code.

What we saw

  • Email is where the difference matters most. The competing app produced plausible but wrong addresses, a "9" for a "g", or "travet" for "travel", that would bounce without warning. CardKeep read all 11 correctly.
  • Labels and badges are not people. The competing app named one contact "Toll-Free Us" and turned a hotel's membership badges into a person called "Fine Hotel". CardKeep left the name empty on a card that printed no person.
  • Phone numbers were CardKeep's weak spot. The AI read every number correctly, but CardKeep's own formatting then mishandled the UK "(0)", one "(+)1" number and US toll-free numbers printed on foreign cards. Those fixes are in the next beta build, and we will repeat this test on it.
  • Neither app was perfect. Only 2 of CardKeep's 11 cards, and none of the competing app's, needed no correction at all. That is why CardKeep highlights uncertain fields and never saves without your review.
About this test. Eleven cards is a small sample from one industry, scanned by one person, so small differences mean little. The test measures first-pass reading only, not speed, sync or ease of use. The competing app is not named; results reflect its free tier on that date. Values were scored as correct when right apart from capitals, accents or punctuation, a country code added to a number that printed none, or one of two names or properties printed on the same card.
